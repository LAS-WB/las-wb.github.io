Party Web Readiness Check

The short answer

A credible baseline of declared web presence. AI readiness remains unproven.

The repository’s core finding makes sense when it describes what its reader retrieved. Our checks support some examples and reveal important gaps. Neither the study nor these website tests establish whether an AI assistant can answer voters’ questions.

1. The headline holds under the study’s rules

We reproduced the categories for all 527 candidates from the saved input flags, with no differences. The reported 391 (74.2%) without qualifying retrieved content includes 313 in the “no declared address” category. It is not a finding that three quarters of websites fail, or that those candidates are invisible to AI.

The rules are explicit, but “readable” uses a word-count threshold and ownership rules. That does not establish useful answers. Missing original page captures also prevent a full replay of the earlier extraction. Source-study assessment · Saved audit

2. LAS corroborates individual observations, not the headline percentage

The PCO article supplied its inspected text without JavaScript. Paula Belmonte’s address again refused the request; Riedel’s communities address again returned “not found.” These support specific earlier observations.

But twelve deliberately varied addresses cannot validate a result for the whole cohort. Orleans Brandao’s browser capture contained text despite an empty plain-text extraction: “our reader got nothing” is narrower than “there is no information.” Dates, networks and extraction scopes differ, so changed results do not by themselves disprove the earlier study.

3. These tests are useful diagnostics, with substantial blind spots

They show whether text was retrieved, whether browser rendering changed the extraction, and whether sampled PDFs contain text. They also identify where to investigate further. Full LAS and “Can AI read it?” are two views of the same scans, not independent replications.

Some checks inspected only a small article section; 11 of 12 click checks were unmeasured; PDF inspection was partial. Visually hidden text may still be readable from the page code. LAS’s service-application scores are not meaningful measures of campaign-site quality. These limitations prevent a general “AI can/cannot read this site” verdict. What each run establishes

4. The missing evidence is whether people get useful, accurate answers

The reviewed repository has 1,584 model responses, all still uncoded, and no completed consumer-assistant observations. Our website probes ran no AI question-answering tests. Neither evidence set establishes answer accuracy or that website structure causes better or worse answers.

Use this work as a documented baseline and a guide to further checks. Stronger AI-readiness claims need complete content inspection and evaluated answers to real voter questions, with supporting sources.

How this check was done

We audited the saved data and category rules in party-web-ai-readiness at its 13 September 2026 snapshot. We then selected twelve addresses for variation—readable candidate pages, sparse responses, errors, PDF links and party articles—and scanned them from a residential connection in Lisbon on 14 September.

Each address produced a full LAS report and a smaller structural report from the same run: 12 scans and 24 reports. These were automated website checks, with no AI model calls. Ten requested pages returned HTTP 200, one returned 403 and one returned 404. Completion means a report was produced, not that every check succeeded.

Counts describe the selected page region, not necessarily the whole page. Click tests block requests that could change state; nine stopped during page loading, and two could not assess error pages. Named-crawler probes used user-agent labels from our connection, not verified vendor infrastructure. PDF samples covered three pages per file. The full LAS reports retain government-service wording and scores, which do not judge campaign information quality.
